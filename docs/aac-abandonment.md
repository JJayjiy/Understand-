# Why AAC gets abandoned

Notes for the DeLuca conversation, and for deciding what CoHear should be.

---

## Correct the number I gave you

I have been telling you **29.3% abandonment, top predictor = lack of user involvement in device selection.** That figure comes from Phillips & Zhao (1993), which studied **assistive technology broadly** — wheelchairs, hearing aids, mobility aids. It is real and widely cited, but it is not an AAC number.

The AAC-specific number is much worse.

**Johnson, Inglebret, Jones & Ray (2006)** surveyed 275 ASHA AAC specialists. The mean proportion of clients still using an AAC system more than a year after their SLP introduced it: **39.35%.**

**Roughly six in ten AAC systems are abandoned or rejected within a year.**

Use the 39% figure with DeLuca. It's the one from her world, and it's more striking than the one I had you using.

---

## What the research says causes it

Johnson et al. ran factor analysis on what SLPs identified. Five constructs came out associated with abandonment:

**1. Not maintaining or adjusting the system**
The single most cited. A device is configured once, at fitting, and then the person changes — new vocabulary, new setting, new motor ability, new school — and nobody updates it. It stops fitting and gets dropped.

**2. Attitude**
Of everyone around the person: parents, teachers, aides, peers. If the people in the room don't believe in it or find it awkward, it dies regardless of how well it works.

**3. Lack of training**
Not of the user — of the *communication partners*. Nobody taught the family, the aide, the teacher how to be on the other end of it.

**4. Lack of support**
No one to call when something breaks or a question comes up. The SLP moves on to the next caseload.

**5. Poor fit**
Wrong device for that person's abilities, setting, or life.

The mirror-image constructs for *success* were **Support, Attitude, and System Fit** — which tells you the same thing from the other side: this is a social problem at least as much as a technical one.

### The parent side

Later work on parent acceptance found six themes: how parents view their child, their beliefs about AAC, their support network, **the capacity and demands on the parent**, the services their SLP provides, and features of the system itself.

That fourth one matters. A parent already managing therapy, school, appointments and a job is being asked to learn and maintain another system. Capacity is finite.

### A reframing worth knowing about

There's a 2023 paper in *AAC* arguing abandonment is the wrong frame entirely — that calling it "abandonment" puts the failure on the user, when often the device simply stopped enabling anything the person actually wanted to do. It proposes a capability-based model instead.

Worth mentioning to DeLuca if it comes up. It signals you've read past the first result.

## What this means for CoHear

Reading the five factors against what you've built:

| Abandonment factor | Where CoHear lands |
|---|---|
| Not maintaining/adjusting | **Good.** Nothing to configure. No vocabulary to keep current, no layout to rebuild. The model improves by you updating it, not by the family doing anything. |
| Lack of training | **Good.** One button. Nothing for a partner to learn. |
| Poor fit | **Unknown.** No one has used it. This is the thing you can't answer. |
| Attitude | **Unknown, and not yours to control.** Depends on whether a phone on the table feels natural or feels like a device. |
| Lack of support | **Weak.** You're one person. There's no one to call. |

**The strongest thing you have is that #1 doesn't apply to you.** The most-cited cause of AAC abandonment — a system that's configured once and then never updated as the person changes — is structurally absent from your design. There is nothing to maintain.

That's not a small point, and it's the one DeLuca will recognize immediately.

**The honest weakness is support.** A clinic can hand a family a device and be there in six weeks. You can't. Worth being upfront about if partnership comes up.

---

## How to use this tomorrow

Don't recite the literature at her. Use it to make one question sharper:

> "The AAC research says something like six in ten systems get abandoned within a year, and the most cited reason is that a system gets configured once and then nobody updates it as the person changes. My tool has nothing to configure — which I think removes that failure mode, but probably introduces others I can't see. Does that match what you watch happen?"

That does three things at once: shows you read her field, offers a specific claim she can correct, and asks about *failure* rather than asking her to admire your design.
