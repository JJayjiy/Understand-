# FCSN families — how to actually get testers

This is the relationship you cannot afford to damage. Four years of trust, an Outstanding Volunteer Award, and weekly access no other student founder has. Move slowly and through the front door.

---

## The honest problem first

Most FCSN students are autistic. **Your model is trained on dysarthria — motor weakness from cerebral palsy and ALS — which is a different mechanism.** Autism-related speech differences involve apraxia (motor *planning*) and prosody, and apraxic errors are characteristically *inconsistent*. Your approach depends on consistency. It may not help them.

Don't hide this. It's the thing that makes parents trust you, and it's genuinely what you need to find out.

**One thing to establish first:** ask the coordinator whether any students have cerebral palsy or other conditions affecting the *physical production* of speech. Those students are true dysarthria cases and are your best fit. You may not know the actual mix.

---

## The sequence — don't skip steps

### Step 1: The coordinator, before any parent

Talk to the program coordinator or director first. Going around the organization to families is the one move that could cost you FCSN permanently.

> "Something I built came out of teaching here, and I'd like to do it properly and with your oversight. I'm not asking to work with students yet — I'd like your read on it first, and if it seems worthwhile, I'd want any family contact to go through you rather than from me directly."

Ask for: their blessing, help identifying which students might fit, and permission for materials to go home through the organization.

### Step 2: Parents try it themselves — no child involved

**This is the key move, and it's the one people skip.**

Ask parents to try the tool *on their own voice*, and then to judge, from their own knowledge of their child: would this have helped? Would the output have been what their kid meant?

Why this is better than testing with children:

- **No consent complexity.** An adult trying a web page requires nothing.
- **No child feels experimented on.** That risk is real and it's the thing that could hurt your relationship.
- **Parents are the best judges anyway.** They've spent years being the person who understands their child when no one else does. They know what a good clarification looks like.
- **You get honest signal fast** — this week, not after weeks of paperwork.

You may learn everything you need without a single child participating.

### Step 3: Only if a parent offers — the child tries

If a parent says "I'd like [name] to try it," then and only then:

- Written parental consent, signed before anything is recorded
- The child's assent too — if they seem uncomfortable, stop immediately
- Speaker codes, never names
- Nothing recorded unless the parent watches it happen
- Delete on request, no questions

**Never initiate this step yourself.** Let it come from them.

---

## What to say to parents

Short, honest, low-pressure. Through the organization, not cold.

> Hi — I'm Nick, I teach the LEGO robotics class at FCSN. I've been there about four years.
>
> Something I've noticed teaching is how often my students have plenty to say and aren't understood. So my project partner and I built a tool that tries to make hard-to-understand speech clearer — it listens to a recording and produces a cleaner version, with the speaker always reviewing it before anything is shared.
>
> **I honestly don't know if it would help your child.** It was trained mostly on speech affected by cerebral palsy and ALS, which works differently from what a lot of our students experience. I'd rather find that out than assume.
>
> What would help me most: would you be willing to try it yourself — on your own voice — and tell me whether the result looks like something that would have helped? It takes about five minutes, nothing is recorded or saved, and it doesn't involve your child at all.
>
> No pressure at all, and it's completely fine to say no. I'm still going to be there teaching robotics either way.

**Why this works:**

- Identifies you as the robotics teacher, not a stranger with a product
- "I honestly don't know if it would help your child" — nobody selling something says that
- The ask is five minutes of *their* time, not their child's
- "Nothing is recorded or saved" removes the biggest fear
- The last line matters most: it makes saying no genuinely free

---

## Questions to ask parents (worth more than the test itself)

1. **"When someone doesn't understand [name], what usually happens next?"** — the real cost, in their words.
2. **"Who understands them best, and what do they do differently?"** — the best listeners have already solved part of your problem.
3. **"Is it more that people can't catch the sounds, or that they can't follow what they mean?"** — separates your Stage 1 from Stage 2 and tells you which product you're building.
4. **"Have you tried any apps or devices? What made you stop?"** — abandonment reasons beat feature requests every time.
5. **"If a tool like this existed and worked, when would you actually use it?"** — reveals whether the moment you're designing for is the moment that matters.

Write answers down verbatim. Quotes are worth more than counts.

---

## What not to do

- **Don't record anything at a session** without organizational approval and signed consent, no matter how well it's going.
- **Don't approach parents in the parking lot.** Through the organization only.
- **Don't promise it will help.** You don't know that, and overpromising to a parent of a child with a disability is a particular kind of harm.
- **Don't push after a no.** One ask, then let it go entirely.

---

## What success looks like in three weeks

- Coordinator informed and supportive
- 3–5 parents have tried it themselves and told you what they think
- You know whether any students have CP or dysarthria specifically
- Two or three quotes about what being misunderstood actually costs a family
- Possibly one family who volunteered for their child to try it

That's real, and none of it requires a single child to be recorded.
