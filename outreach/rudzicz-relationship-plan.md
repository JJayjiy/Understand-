# Frank Rudzicz — who he is, and how to build a relationship

## Who you're actually talking to

He is considerably more senior than "the TORGO guy."

- **Professor**, Faculty of Computer Science, **Dalhousie University** (Halifax)
- **Associate Professor**, Computer Science, **University of Toronto**
- **Canada CIFAR AI Chair**; faculty member, **Vector Institute**
- **Co-founder, WinterLight Labs** — speech-based cognitive assessment, successful exit. Founded with two of his own students.
- **President of the joint ACL/ISCA Special Interest Group on Speech and Language Processing for Assistive Technologies (SLPAT)** — since 2015
- Recipient, **Excellence in Applied Research**, Speech-Language & Audiology Canada
- Mentor at **Creative Destruction Lab**
- Quoted in the New York Times, Wired, Nature, Scientific American

His PhD thesis was *Production Knowledge in the Recognition of Dysarthric Speech*. **TORGO came out of that thesis.** You didn't email a corpus maintainer; you emailed the person whose doctoral work created the dataset your project is built on, who then spent twenty years becoming the leading figure in this exact subfield.

He is also still active in it — he published on dysarthric severity assessment in 2026.

## What that means for an ask

**The honest read: don't ask for collaboration now.**

Not because you don't deserve to, and not because he'd be rude about it. Because of what just happened between you.

He gave you conditional permission. You met the conditions. The natural next beat is you showing him it's done — nothing else. If you attach a collaboration request to that same email, you convert a clean interaction into a transaction, and you spend the goodwill on the least valuable thing you could buy with it.

Also, consider the realistic answer to "will you collaborate with me." He's a full professor, a CIFAR chair, and a serial founder, and you're a high school senior three time zones away in another country. Even if he likes the work, "collaboration" has no shape he could say yes to. **Vague asks get polite non-answers, and a polite non-answer closes a door that's currently open.**

## What's actually realistic

| Ask | Realistic? |
|---|---|
| Answering a specific technical question | **Yes** — he already did once, quickly |
| Pointing you toward a venue or a person | **Yes** |
| Reading a draft if you've earned it | Maybe, later |
| Informal advisor over months | Possible, if you keep delivering |
| Formal collaboration, co-authorship, lab position | No — not now, and not from a cold ask |

## The sequence that actually works

**1. Send the release email as drafted. No ask.**

It already contains the seed: the `.PHN` provenance question. That's a real question, it affects a number you're publishing, and he is one of very few people who can answer it. If he replies, he has voluntarily re-entered the conversation — which is worth far more than a request you extracted.

**2. If he answers, close the loop fast.**

Correct the number, tell him you corrected it, one or two sentences. This is the move almost nobody makes, and it's what separates someone worth answering again from someone who was just collecting a reply.

**3. Run the leave-one-speaker-out experiment.**

You owe it, you've said so publicly in your write-up, and it's the obvious next result. When it's done, send him the outcome — *especially if it contradicts your current finding.* "You were right to be skeptical, here's what happened" is the single most credibility-building email a junior person can send a senior one.

**4. Then, and only then, ask something specific.**

Not "would you like to collaborate." Something with a shape:

> "I'm planning to submit this to SLPAT. Would you be willing to tell me whether the framing is sound before I do?"

Specific, bounded, easy to say yes to, and it costs him twenty minutes instead of an open-ended commitment.

## The venue you should know about

**He is president of SLPAT** — the ACL/ISCA special interest group for speech and language processing in assistive technology. That is precisely, exactly the field your project sits in, and he has run it since 2015.

Getting your work into that community is a better goal than getting Frank personally attached to it. It puts you in his world without asking him for anything, and it's the kind of thing that makes a later ask trivial to answer.

Look up SLPAT's current workshop cycle and deadlines. Your control-calibration finding — that 27% of apparent phonological signal is measurement noise, and one phone points backwards — is genuinely the kind of methodological result that community cares about.

## Two practical constraints

**He's in Canada.** Dalhousie is in Halifax. Anything with him is remote.

**You're a minor.** Formal involvement with a university lab likely runs into HR and ethics-approval friction that has nothing to do with whether your work is good. Informal correspondence has none of that friction, which is another reason to keep it there for now.

## The thing worth remembering

A senior professor answered a high school student's cold email quickly, engaged with the substance, and gave specific constructive conditions rather than a form refusal. That already tells you what kind of person he is.

The way to use that is to be someone who reliably does what he says he'll do, reports honestly including the parts that went badly, and doesn't ask for more than the relationship currently holds. Do that two or three times and you won't need to ask for collaboration — it'll just be what's happening.
