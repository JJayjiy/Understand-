## Two things to get right in the first three minutes

### 1. Correct "aphasia" → dysarthria

Marcella has said "aphasia app" twice. It's wrong, and DeLuca will know instantly. Say it early, lightly, and move on:

> "One quick clarification before we start — Marcella described it as an aphasia app, but it's actually for dysarthria. Motor speech, not language."

Don't belabor it. One sentence, then go.

### 2. Know the thing about hemiplegia before she has to tell you

**Roughly half of children with CP have dysarthria, but it's least common in hemiplegic CP** — the population her CIMT work targets. It's most common in dyskinetic CP, and common in spastic quadriplegia.

So the overlap with her *CIMT patients specifically* is thinner than the introduction implied. If you walk in assuming her kids need your app, she'll be too polite to correct you and the meeting will drift.

**Say it yourself, as a question:**

> "I know speech is often relatively spared in hemiplegic CP compared with dyskinetic or quadriplegic presentations. Does dysarthria come up much in your clinic population, or would I be looking at a different group?"

That single question does three things: shows you read about her field, makes her an expert rather than a target, and gets you the honest answer you need. It's the best opening you have.

**Her value to you is her network and judgment, not her patient list.** She co-directs a neuromotor clinic and sits on AACPDM's committee — she knows everyone in pediatric CP. That's the resource.

## The one question worth the whole meeting

CIMT's core principle is *forced use*: constrain the stronger limb so the impaired one has to work. Don't let the child compensate, because compensation prevents the practice that drives plasticity.

**Your app is a compensation.** If a tool makes a child easier to understand, does that reduce the pressure to keep working on speech?

> "CIMT is built on forced use — you constrain the good arm so the impaired one has to work. My app does the opposite for speech: it makes the person easier to understand without them changing anything. Should I be worried that it reduces the practice that drives improvement? Is there a version of this that's more like a therapy tool than a workaround?"

This is genuinely the risk you can't evaluate alone, it's precisely her expertise, and asking it before she does is the difference between a student showing off a project and a person doing serious work.

Ask it about *your* product, not her field. You're asking her to find the flaw, not defending against one.

**On the population**

**On what already exists**

**On getting it in front of people**

## Careful with these

**Don't ask to "touch base every other week."** That's a standing commitment from a full professor to a stranger, and it invites a polite no. Instead:

> "Would it be alright if I sent you an update when I have something new — maybe once a month?"

Low cost, easy yes, and she can escalate it herself if she wants to.

**Don't ask for a letter, an advisor role, or a partnership today.** First meeting. Let her offer.

**Don't oversell.** She runs randomized controlled trials. Eight speakers, no human subjects, one severe speaker in the headline number — she will see that immediately. Saying it first is the only move that works.

**Don't use "medical," "clinical," "therapy," or "treatment"** to describe what you built. It's a listener aid and a research artifact.
